The big picture

Oil climbed back to its war highs, Indian small shares had their worst week in 20 months, and yet most company results were good.

In short
  • Attacks on Saudi oil sites pushed Brent crude back up to about $117 a barrel.
  • Costlier oil keeps Indian inflation and borrowing costs high, so borrowed money left small shares fast.
  • Under the falling prices, most September-quarter results were strong, and Indian funds kept buying.

One cause behind most of the pain

The war around Iran is in its 9th month, and the shipping lane at the Strait of Hormuz (the narrow sea exit that carries about a fifth of the world's oil) is still not normal. When Yemen's Houthi fighters hit Saudi oil sites, the oil price jumped again.

Dear oil reaches India through a bigger import bill, higher prices in shops and higher interest rates. All of these showed up in the week to 23 October. Indian inflation is at its highest in almost 2 years, the Reserve Bank raised its rate earlier in October, and the 10-year government bond yield (the yearly return lenders ask for to lend to the government for that long) has now risen for 10 weeks in a row.

Why small shares fell the most

Many small investors bought small and mid-sized shares with borrowed money through margin trading (a loan from the broker against the shares). When prices fall, brokers ask for more money, and people who cannot pay are forced to sell. That forced selling hit the smallest companies hardest.

The big indices hide how deep the fall is. The Nifty 50 is only about 9% below its peak by one count, but the typical small company is down about 44% from its own high.

The part the price screen does not show

Results for the July-September quarter (called Q2 FY27) were mostly good. Banks grew loans by about 20%, and many factory, power and defence-electronics companies posted record numbers. One view is that good results, cheap prices and very bad mood together usually mark a bottom. The opposite view is that oil and interest rates decide the next move, not profits, and both are still rising.

Reading between the lines, the same war is behind both the selling and some of the buying. It raises India's costs, but it also fills order books for power equipment, defence and Indian oil refiners, which is why a few sectors kept rising while the rest fell.

The thread that ties this issue together

Another worry came from America. Investors started to doubt that the huge spending on AI data centres will pay back soon, and AI-related shares fell sharply. That pushed money into US government bonds, which lowered US interest rates even as oil rose. Sections 03 and 09 explain this unusual pairing.

02

Oil, ships and the war

Strikes on Saudi oil sites pushed crude to its highest since March, and diesel is hurting more than crude itself.

In short
  • Brent crude rose to about $117 after Houthi attacks inside Saudi Arabia.
  • Diesel in Europe is near $200 a barrel, above the peaks of 2008 and 2022.
  • America's emergency oil stock is the lowest in 44 years, so it has little left to calm prices.

What happened to oil

Debris from a Houthi drone fell near Saudi Arabia's Shaybah oil plant, and another attack hit the airport at Dammam. Earlier strikes had already stopped the East-West pipeline, the line that lets Saudi oil skip the Strait of Hormuz and reach the Red Sea.

Brent crude, $ per barrel, 21-23 October

Points from a Barchart chart, The National, ZeroHedge and HFI Research posts.

Brent jumped about $9 in 2 days and ended the week only about $2 below its high for the year.

  • Blockade — the US naval blockade of Iran has run for more than 100 days; Iran says it has cut imports at its southern ports by 25%.
  • Borders — Pakistan and Iraq shut some land crossings with Iran, squeezing its trade further.
  • Threats — Iran says that if bombing restarts it will hit airports and pipelines in Gulf countries; the US is moving some diplomats out of the region.
  • Stalemate — America's UN ambassador, Mike Waltz, said the US could still be stuck a year from now, with Hormuz shipping not back to normal.
  • At sea — a tanker was hit by an unknown projectile near Oman, and an oil spill from an attacked tanker closed beaches in the UAE.

How long the Saudi bypass will stay broken

The East-West pipeline was hit at 3 pumping stations and at the Yanbu export terminal. One view, from a security analyst who has worked in those deserts, is that repairs take 1 to 3 months or longer, because the stations must be rebuilt from the ground and skilled foreign workers have left. The opposite view, common on social media, was that flows would return within days.

Reading between the lines, every week the bypass stays shut forces more Saudi oil toward Hormuz, which is exactly the route Iran is threatening. That is why traders keep paying a war premium, put at more than $20 a barrel by Goldman Sachs.

Diesel is the real squeeze

Crude is the raw input, but trucks, farms and factories run on diesel. Europe has closed so many refineries that it now imports much of its fuel.

  • Europe — diesel is near $195-200 a barrel; refining capacity is down 45% since its 1976 peak while population rose 13%.
  • America — diesel averages $6.30 a gallon at the pump.
  • Flows — a Barclays note says Middle East oil flows are 84% of pre-war levels, and stocks have fallen by more than 500 million barrels.
  • India — the same note says India now imports about 600,000 barrels a day more crude and fuel than a year ago.

Views split on what comes next. One view is that refining margins and tanker rates have already peaked, so every further rise in crude eats into refiners until they cut runs, and demand falls. The opposite view points to air travel still near record highs: the world is short of about 10 million barrels a day, far more than normal belt-tightening can save, and the International Energy Agency counts 60 countries already rationing fuel.

03

The price of money

Indian bond yields rose for a 10th straight week while US yields fell, and gold slipped below $4,000.

In short
  • India's 10-year bond yield is at 7.47%, after its longest run of weekly rises in about 30 years.
  • US yields fell instead, because scared investors moved from AI shares into safe government bonds.
  • Gold and silver fell hard, even though the war continues.

India: borrowing keeps getting dearer

A rising bond yield means lenders want more interest. That pushes up loan rates for companies, home buyers and the government itself.

India 10-year yield7.47%Up 10 weeks in a row, the worst streak in about 30 years
Repo rate5.50%Raised by 0.25% on 8 October, the RBI's main lending rate
Retail inflation5.5%September, a 23-month high, up from 4.8%
Rupee94.94Per dollar on 23 October; touched 94.30 a day earlier

The Reserve Bank's own monthly review says price rises are now spreading across many goods, which calls for a matching change in policy. In plain words, it is leaving the door open to more rate rises. IDFC First Bank is already planning for 2 more increases of 0.25% each.

  • Cash in the system — currency in circulation grew 13.5%, the fastest in 5 years, helped by $133 billion that banks raised in special dollar deposits (FCNR(B)) through an RBI swap window that closed on 31 August.
  • Liquidity — the RBI is now draining that cash, and an economist at IDBI Bank expects money to get tighter in the festival season.
  • Reserves — India's foreign exchange reserves fell to $721 billion, down $65 billion in 6 weeks from a record, as the RBI sold dollars to steady the rupee.
  • Dollars for oil — dealers say SBI has been buying dollars for oil companies on the RBI's behalf, so that big oil payments do not hit the market all at once.

Reading between the lines, the special dollar deposits explain much of the pain. They let banks lend more without raising rupee deposits, but now the money is being pulled back just as oil bills rise. That is why India's yields rise even on days when US yields fall.

America: oil went up, but yields went down

Normally, dearer oil means higher inflation and higher bond yields. On 22 October that link broke. The US 10-year yield fell to 4.95%, its biggest one-day drop in more than 3 years, as investors sold AI shares and bought bonds.

  • Bets — buyers of call options on long-dated US bonds now pay the most since March 2025; a single trade, costing about $7 million, bets the 10-year yield falls to 4.60%.
  • Fed — markets still put the chance of another US rate rise in December at about 88%.
  • Shoppers — the University of Michigan mood index is 48, near record lows, and people expect 4.7% inflation over the next year.
  • Gap with India — India's bonds paid 6.3 points more than US bonds in 2012; the gap is now about 2.1 points.

One view is that falling US yields will help India, because foreign money finds Indian bonds more attractive. The opposite view is that the gap is now so thin that foreign investors gain little by coming to India, which is one reason they sold about ₹48,000 crore of Indian shares in October.

The big indices fell modestly, but the typical small share is down by nearly half, and margin loans are being pulled.

In short
  • Smallcap shares had their worst week in 20 months, falling about 7%.
  • Forced selling of shares bought with broker loans made the fall sharper.
  • At the same time, Jio Platforms drew the biggest demand of any Indian share sale ever.

  • Wealth lost — about ₹21 lakh crore of market value vanished over the week, 4.4% of everything listed.
  • Breadth — on 23 October, 1,356 shares fell 3% or more within the first hour, the 3rd-worst selling day of the year.
  • Fear gauge — India VIX rose for a 4th session to 16.84.

Over the whole week, even large companies fell hard. Adani Enterprises, Shriram Finance and Nestle India lost close to 9%, while Sun Pharma and Divi's barely moved, so medicine makers acted as the safe corner.

Borrowed money is leaving

Margin trading funding (MTF) lets an investor pay part of a share's price and borrow the rest from the broker. The total of these loans fell to about ₹1.4 lakh crore, well down from a few days earlier, and the unwinding has now run for 3 straight weeks, the first such run since March.

One view is that the MTF book is still close to its peak, so if even a third of it is sold, panic spreads; this view also says the regulator tightened rules on options but left margin loans loose. The opposite view is that a falling MTF book means the forced sellers are nearly done.

  • Where traders went — after a higher tax on index options, traders moved to commodity options; options on MCX now trade a larger face value than on America's CME.
  • Money going abroad — Indians sent $321.5 million overseas to buy foreign shares and bonds in August, 111% more than a year earlier; that is about ₹1 abroad for every ₹10 put into SIPs.

India's economy, trade and policy

Business activity is strong and trade deals are lining up, but firms are not hiring, and policy shifts are moving money between sectors.

In short
  • Factories and services are busy, but companies are adding very few jobs.
  • Deals with Europe, Germany and America are moving forward at the same time.
  • New rules on ethanol and rooftop solar shift profits from one group of companies to another.

Export orders grew at their fastest in 6 months, led by the US, the UK and West Asia. But service firms stopped adding staff for the first time in 9 months. Reading between the lines, companies are squeezing more work out of the people and machines they already have, partly with AI tools, which lifts profit but not jobs.

What India buys and sells

  • Electronics — a record 20% of India's imports in September, up from 14% a year ago; electronics imports rose 45% to $81.3 billion in April-September.
  • Gold — its share of imports fell to 2.4% from 14%, after duty rises and other curbs.
  • Engineering exports — above $70 billion in April-September, up nearly 20%; the target is $250 billion by 2030.
  • Apple — its India revenue grew 32% to ₹1,04,607 crore in the year to March 2026.

So India's phone exports still rest on imported parts. The jump in electronics imports is the cost of assembling here before the parts are made here.

Energy rules that move money

Oil companies invited bids for about 1,100 crore litres of ethanol for November 2026 to October 2027, with a big change. They will not buy ethanol made from sugarcane juice or B-heavy molasses at all, only from grains such as rice and maize, or from C-heavy molasses.

So sugar mills lose an income stream they had built over years, while grain-based distillers gain. Maize and rice prices may rise, and an estimate says the switch could add ₹813 crore to oil companies' buying costs.

  • Rooftop solar — more than 5 million homes have panels under PM Surya Ghar, about 14.9 GW in all; total rooftop solar is 30.7 GW, 19% of India's solar.
  • The billing problem — homes sell midday power worth about ₹1.56 a unit but get full credit against evening power that costs distribution companies up to ₹10; the fix suggested is time-of-day pricing plus home batteries.
  • Solar cells — Vikram Solar will start making cells at its 9 GW Tamil Nadu plant in December.
  • Haryana power — a strike by electricity workers ended after the state promised no private distribution licences, though the state is short of power.

Defence and the border

  • Exports — a record ₹38,424 crore; the Defence Secretary says India is now the 3rd-largest arms exporter to Europe, mainly artillery.
  • Private makers — about 17 companies are taking DRDO missile technology, and startups with proven prototypes get 5 years of assured orders.

06

Banks, lenders and fund houses

Banks grew fast and kept bad loans low, but special dollar deposits flatter the growth, and gold loans need watching.

In short
  • Bank loans grew about 20% a year, and private banks grew profit much faster than state banks.

Gold loans: safe or stretched?

Gold loans are India's 2nd-largest kind of retail loan after home loans. With gold down about 15% from its August high, the value backing each loan has shrunk.

  • South Indian Bank — its gold loan book grew 41% to ₹26,603 crore; its chief says borrowers borrow 70-75% of the gold's value, leaving a 20-25% cushion, and Indians do not walk away from their gold.
  • Piramal Finance — its chief says 15-23% of the industry's gold loans in 2025-26, worth about ₹4.7 lakh crore, went to people already more than 90 days late on another loan.
  • Bajaj Finance — loans 31-90 days overdue rose, and ₹1,047 crore of the rise came from new short-term gold loan schemes.
  • Shriram Finance — its monthly-instalment gold loans "have not worked well", and bad gold loans rose to 3.12% from 2.57%.
  • Jana Small Finance Bank — gold lending slowed as falling prices led to margin calls on customers.

Reading between the lines, both sides can be right. Old-style gold loans, repaid in one go, look safe; the newer monthly-instalment products sold to stretched borrowers are where stress is showing.

Rural caution

Shriram Finance told analysts it is cutting its growth guidance to 16-17% and raising expected credit losses to 2.3%, because poor rain in parts of Karnataka, Maharashtra and central India may hurt rural borrowers late in the year. A lending analyst sees the microfinance pain as over, but says small home loans to low-income buyers are now hurting.

Fund houses: more money, thinner fees

  • SBI Funds Management — the largest fund house, with a 15% market share, but profit fell 6% and monthly SIP inflows grew only 3%.
  • Nippon Life India AMC — core profit rose 25%, but its SIP book grew 2% against 11% for the industry, and its fee on equity funds slipped to 0.52% from 0.54%.
  • UTI AMC — equity assets slightly down from a year ago, while rivals grew.

Cheap index funds and ETFs are taking a larger share of new money; ETFs are already 32% of Nippon's fund assets. So fund houses can grow assets and still see fees per rupee shrink.

Indian IT and the AI squeeze

Infosys cut its forecast because AI makes clients demand lower prices, while Coforge showed how AI can lift margins instead.

In short

Infosys: more AI, less growth

Infosys lowered its revenue forecast for the year, measured in constant currency (growth with exchange-rate moves removed). Profit missed estimates, and the cut came after the share had rallied about 17% in October.

  • Forecast — growth of 1-2% for the year, down from 1.5-3%; quarterly profit was ₹7,838 crore, up 6.3%.
  • Why — expected price rises did not come, volumes were softer, and retail and hi-tech clients cut spending; retail revenue fell 7.7% and hi-tech 17.3%.
  • AI deflation — chief executive Salil Parekh said clients' demands for AI savings are spreading across more contracts, and older contracts adjust when renewed.
  • AI growth — AI work is 10.5% of revenue, up from 8.2% in June, and grew 29% in the quarter.
  • Next year — a new 7-year banking contract worth over $2 billion starts late in the January-March quarter and should add more than 1 point to growth in the next financial year.
  • Cyber — Parekh called the wave of global cyber attacks "a huge opportunity", with Infosys building AI agents to defend clients.

The cyber wave reaches India

Many critical security flaws were disclosed in October, and attackers now use AI tools to find and exploit them quickly. A Goldman Sachs trader even asked whether this cyber wave helped sink AI shares.

  • Banks — SBI has confirmed customer records on the dark web, and Bank of Baroda is dealing with a data breach.

  • Korea — a cyber attack on major South Korean banks may have been the work of 1 person using open AI models and an AI hacking tool, CrowdStrike reported.

So cyber defence is turning from a cost into a growth area. Banks face penalties of up to ₹250 crore under India's data protection law, while firms that sell protection gain.
